\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

Four questions follow: tail latency at fixed expert-memory budgets (RQ1), a shared online assignment against a fixed order and a frozen one (RQ2), which of the three removals in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-mechanisms} moves the Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} gap (RQ3), and whether the online surrogates and the controller are cheap enough to run (RQ4).

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:eval-setup}

\paragraph{Platform and models.}
We evaluate on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48\,GB of device memory. Table~\ref{tab:eval-models} summarizes each checkpoint's routing structure and the two packed tiers of routed experts. For Qwen3-Next, the shared expert is not routed and remains resident with the non-expert weights.

For Qwen3-30B, the expert arena occupies 29.74\,GB, corresponding to \(\PhiRatio=1.931\). The low- and high-tier expert packs occupy 15.40\,GB and 57.98\,GB, respectively, giving \(\rho=3.76\); keeping both packs resident would require 73.38\,GB and therefore exceeds device capacity. Host-to-device bandwidth in the replay is 24.86\,GB/s.

Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} and the later latency figures for this model use \(B=20.0\)\,GB (\(\PhiRatio=1.3\)), a reserve 9.74\,GB larger than the one that leaves 29.74\,GB. Table~\ref{tab:eval-phi07} uses \(B=10.78\)\,GB (\(\PhiRatio=0.7\)).

\paragraph{Workloads and quality.}
Latency experiments use a fixed request order drawn from a mixed workload comprising MMLU-Pro~\cite{wang2024mmlu}, GPQA~\cite{rein2024gpqa}, AIME25~\cite{zhang2025aime25}, GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, and HumanEval~\cite{chen2021evaluating}. We construct workload slices according to the input/output token-length ratio: prefill-heavy requests have the largest input-to-output ratios, decode-heavy requests have the largest output-to-input ratios, and balanced requests minimize the difference between input and output lengths. Unless otherwise specified, inference uses a batch size of 8. We measure model-path time to first token (TTFT) and time per output token (TPOT). Request queueing, tokenization, transport, serialization, offered load, and goodput are outside the measured region.

Calibration prompts determine the sensitivity estimates, predictor width, controller constants, and baseline configurations. Evaluation prompts are disjoint, and no parameter is refit on the test set. \textsc{Static-Joint} keeps the assignment frozen on the calibration mix. \systemname\ may change the assignment on that stream.

The all-high reference executes every routed expert using the model's high-tier representation under the same runtime as \systemname. Experts not currently resident in HBM are streamed through the same residency path used by the evaluated policies. Thus, all-high defines a fidelity reference rather than an unconstrained placement policy.

The scores in Table~\ref{tab:eval-accuracy} use 200 MMLU-Pro items, 198 GPQA items, 30 AIME25 items, 200 GSM8K items, and 164 HumanEval items. Avg.\ weights the five accuracies by these counts.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We organize the baselines by the resource-management decision they isolate. \emph{Uniform-low} places every routed expert at the low tier. \emph{Static-mixed} keeps that low-tier pack resident and spends the slack \(B-P_{\mathrm{lo}}\) on fidelity increments for the largest-\(q_i\) experts that fit, then freezes the assignment. At the Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} budgets \(\Phi\ge 1\), lookahead is off and the measured segment transfers no expert. \emph{Residency-only} leaves the slack idle and \emph{Lookahead-only} stages no expert; both are the Uniform-low point. At the Table~\ref{tab:eval-phi07} budget the low-tier pack does not fit, so Static-mixed adds no fidelity increment and absent experts transfer at low precision. \textsc{Seq-FR} and \textsc{Seq-RF} test whether independently optimizing the same decisions in a fixed order is sufficient: \textsc{Seq-FR} assigns fidelity before residency and lookahead, whereas \textsc{Seq-RF} assigns residency before fidelity and lookahead. \textsc{Static-Joint} jointly assigns \(B\) among fidelity, residency, and lookahead on calibration statistics, keeps the feasible assignment with the lowest latency, and freezes that assignment on the evaluation stream. \emph{Fixed-partition} instead reserves independent quotas for the three uses, testing the value of allowing memory to move across them.

For controlled comparisons, every deployable policy receives the same expert-memory budget \(B\), expert representations, kernels, request stream, and transition interface. Baselines that require calibration receive the same calibration traces and search budget as \systemname. HOBBIT is the complete published system~\cite{tang2024hobbit}, including mixed-precision loading, adaptive prefetching, the multi-level cache, and the low-precision miss path.

\paragraph{Metrics and statistics.}
We report time to first token (TTFT), time per output token (TPOT), and exposed expert-transfer wait. Latency runs use five warm-up iterations followed by 1,000 measured requests on each of five seeds. Policies that share a seed see the same request order. For each policy and seed, the request-level p95 TPOT is computed on that seed's 1,000 requests. The bracket beside a p95 is the 95\% interval of those five seed-level values. TTFT is a point estimate, and the paper reports no interval for it. Comparisons below use the printed centers of these brackets.

The SLO column is the percentage of requests whose TPOT is at most \textsc{Static-Joint}'s p95 TPOT on that model. A p95 line leaves the rest of that policy's own requests above it.

Replay measurements use 1,000 measured trials following two warm-up trials on a fixed WikiText routing trace and report exposed transfer wait.

\paragraph{Memory accounting and admission.}
A run is valid only if arena occupancy remains within the expert-memory budget \(B\) and its exchange log can reconstruct the reported allocation. NVML samples process-level GPU memory every 2\,ms, and Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} reports that high-water mark as Peak.

\subsection{End-to-End Quality--Latency Tradeoff}
\label{sec:eval-end-to-end}

\paragraph{RQ1: Tail latency at fixed expert-memory budgets.}
We evaluate each model at a binding memory budget and compare policies along the quality--latency frontier. A configuration is considered quality-feasible when its perplexity degradation relative to All-high satisfies \(\Delta\mathrm{PPL}\leq\epsilon\). Configurations outside this bound remain visible to expose the latency--quality tradeoff, but are excluded from the feasible latency comparison.

\subsection{Mechanism Attribution}
\label{sec:eval-mechanism}

\paragraph{RQ3: Which of these removals moves the Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} gap?}
We isolate the contribution of \systemname's key mechanisms by removing one at a time while holding the workload, expert-memory budget \(B\), and execution path fixed within each experiment. \emph{Independent-value} makes allocation decisions using each candidate's individual value, without accounting for its effect on subsequent exchanges. \emph{Slack-only} restricts admission to currently available slack and disables donor selection. \emph{Fixed-partition}, defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-setup}, replaces the shared arena with separate quotas for fidelity, residency, and lookahead.

On both panels of Figure~\ref{fig:eval-mechanisms}, Slack-only's centers are 50.8\,ms and 177\,ms, above \systemname's 45.8\,ms and 141\,ms. Fixed-partition's centers are 52.4\,ms and 192\,ms, above \systemname\ and above the dashed \textsc{Static-Joint} centers. The 2.8\,ms and 27\,ms gaps are \systemname\ against the Table~\ref{tab:eval-main} \textsc{Static-Joint} centers, 48.6\,ms and 168\,ms. On these two workloads, removing the cross-exchange term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:value} does not move the p95 center. Removing donor selection or the shared arena does.

\subsection{Surrogate Checks and Runtime Cost}
\label{sec:eval-models}

\paragraph{RQ4: Are the online surrogates accurate and inexpensive enough for runtime decisions?}
\systemname\ relies on approximate signals to make allocation decisions on the critical path. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-validation} checks predicted exposed wait, \(\widehat R\), lookahead recall, and controller time for Qwen3-30B.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-validation}(a) compares the latency surrogate \(\widehat L\) with measured exposed transfer wait. The surrogate estimates the cost of an exchange from the transferred bytes and the execution window available to hide the transfer. On this workload the predictions sit above measured wait, and the panel marks a bias of \(+1.2\)\,ms per token.

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-validation}(b) evaluates \(\widehat R\) against measured \(\Delta\mathrm{PPL}\). Of the states admitted under \(\widehat\epsilon\), 5 of 71 have measured \(\Delta\mathrm{PPL}\) above \(\epsilon\), an unsafe-accept rate of 7.0\%. Their median measured value is \(1.04\epsilon\), and the largest is \(1.12\epsilon\). The panel has 148 states: 66 safe accepts, 5 unsafe accepts, 18 conservative rejects, and 59 rejects. Both axes are divided by their limits. The vertical limit is \(\epsilon=0.70\). The horizontal limit is \(\widehat\epsilon\).

Figure~\ref{fig:eval-validation}(c) measures lookahead recall at lookahead depth 3. Recall is 0.71 on prefill-heavy requests, 0.58 on mixed requests, and 0.46 on decode-heavy requests. Recall is the fraction of the subsequently demanded expert set identified early enough to influence allocation.

Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:eval-validation}(d) measures controller cost as the number of exchange candidates \(K\) and the replay window \(W\) increase. Our default operating point is \(K=8\) and \(W=32\), where replay time is 0.15\,ms, below one layer. The same panel marks a band from 33\,ms to 130\,ms once \(K\) and \(W\) leave that point. We compare controller time with the available execution window to determine whether allocation decisions remain off the critical path, and increase \(K\) and \(W\) to characterize how the optimization cost scales beyond the default configuration.
